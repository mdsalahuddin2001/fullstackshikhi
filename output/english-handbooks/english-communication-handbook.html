English for everyday confidence

A practical handbook for a Bangla speaker

Book 1 · Learn, practise, check, repeat

For someone who already knows basic English and wants to speak, listen, read, and write with greater ease.

বাংলা বুঝে শিখুন, ইংরেজিতে ব্যবহার করুন। Learn with Bangla support; build the habit of using English.

This book gives you a structured course, model conversations, independent practice, and answers. Use it together with Book 2, the vocabulary companion. You do not need another textbook to follow this course.

Your goal: communicate clearly, keep a conversation going, understand the main message, ask for clarification, and express your ideas with useful detail.

An honest promise: finishing a book does not automatically produce fluency. The learning happens when you retrieve language, speak aloud, listen, notice mistakes, and try again. This course contains the material for that practice. Listening requires sound: use a device's English read-aloud feature or a partner for the included scripts.

Suggested pace: 12 weeks, 6 study days per week, 40-50 minutes per day. Repeat weeks when necessary. This is a schedule, not a guaranteed time to fluency.

02 · A diagnostic you can repeat

Do this before studying. Do not look at the answers on page 25. Keep your first recording and writing sample for comparison.

A. Sentence control: 10 points

Write the missing word or correct the sentence. Each item is worth one point.

1. She ___ to work by bus every day. (go / goes / going)

2. I ___ dinner when you called. (cooked / was cooking / cook)

3. I have lived here ___ 2022. (for / since / from)

4. He ___ like coffee. (don't / doesn't / isn't)

5. I need ___ information. (an / some / many)

6. Make a question: “You finished the task.”

7. Correct: “I am agree with you.”

8. Correct: “Please explain me the problem.”

9. Complete: “If it rains tomorrow, we ___ stay home.”

10. Join with because: “I left early. I felt sick.”

B. Writing: 10 minutes

Write 100-130 words about your typical day, something that happened last week, and one plan for next month. Include a reason and an example. Do not use a translator. Underline sentences you are unsure about.

C. Speaking: 2 minutes

Record yourself answering: “What do you do, what are you learning, and why does it matter to you?” Then explain a problem you recently solved. If you stop, use “Let me think” and continue.

D. Listening: 3 minutes

Have a partner or device read Script A on page 20 while you keep the text hidden. Listen twice. Write the place, time, purpose, and action required. Check against the script.

Use the result to choose your pace

Sentence score 0-5: spend extra time on pages 5-11. Score 6-8: follow the full course. Score 9-10: move faster through familiar grammar, but still complete the speaking tasks. These are course-routing guidelines, not a standardized proficiency test.

For speaking and writing, use the rubric on page 29. A strong grammar score alone does not establish fluency.

The five-step practice loop

1. Understand: read the meaning and notice the sentence pattern.

2. Retrieve: cover the example and produce it from the Bangla meaning or situation.

3. Personalize: replace the people, place, time, or reason with your own information.

4. Use: put the phrase in a short conversation, voice note, or message.

5. Review: revisit it tomorrow, after 3 days, after 7 days, and after 14 days. Review sooner if you forget.

This review schedule is a practical starting point. Adjust it to what you actually remember.

05 · Build a complete sentence

English usually puts the verb before the object

Bangla often follows subject + object + verb: আমি চা খাই। English usually follows subject + verb + object: “I drink tea.” Translate the meaning, then build the English order.

PatternExampleBangla meaning
Subject + verbThe baby sleeps.বাচ্চাটি ঘুমায়।
Subject + verb + objectI need help.আমার সাহায্য দরকার।
Subject + be + descriptionI am ready.আমি প্রস্তুত।
Subject + be + placeThe keys are on the table.চাবিগুলো টেবিলের ওপর আছে।
There is / are + nounThere is a problem.একটি সমস্যা আছে।
It + be + descriptionIt is difficult to decide.সিদ্ধান্ত নেওয়া কঠিন।

English normally needs a subject. Say “It is raining,” not “Is raining.” Use it for weather or a situation; use there is/are to introduce something that exists.

Be is a verb; do not add it everywhere

“I am tired” describes a state with an adjective. “I work here” uses the action verb work. “I am working” uses be + -ing for an activity in progress. “I am agree” is incorrect because agree already functions as a verb: “I agree.”

Expand a short sentence

“I study.” → “I study English.” → “I study English at home.” → “I study English at home every evening because I want to communicate more confidently.”

Add information in manageable pieces: who, what, where, when, why. Do not force every detail into one long sentence.

Pronouns you need to control

I → me → my; you → you → your; he → him → his; she → her → her; it → it → its; we → us → our; they → them → their.

“She helped me.” “This is my notebook.” “The notebook is mine.” Possessive pronouns: mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs. “It's” means “it is” or “it has”; “its” shows possession.

Try it: translate “আমি প্রতিদিন বাসায় ইংরেজি অনুশীলন করি।” Then add a reason. Say five true sentences using five different patterns above. Model: “I practise English at home every day because I want to speak more clearly.”

06 · Talk about habits and what is happening

Present simple: habits, facts, and regular situations

“I work in Dhaka.” “She works from home.” “We usually eat at eight.” With he/she/it, usually add -s or -es: works, watches, goes. Have becomes has.

Negative: “I don't work on Fridays.” “She doesn't work on Fridays.” After doesn't, use the base verb: doesn't work, not doesn't works.

Question: “Do you work here?” “Does he speak English?” Be behaves differently: “Are you ready?” “She isn't ready.” Do not say “Do you are ready?”

Present continuous: now or a temporary situation

Use am/is/are + verb-ing: “I am studying now.” “She is staying with her sister this week.” Negative: “We aren't waiting.” Question: “Are you listening?”

Compare: “I work at a bank” describes my usual job. “I'm working from home today” describes today's arrangement.

Verbs such as know, need, understand, and believe are usually simple when they express a state: “I understand,” not “I am understanding.” Some verbs change meaning: “I think it's useful” is an opinion; “I'm thinking about it” is a mental activity.

Frequency words

Always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never. They usually go before the main verb but after be: “I often walk.” “She is usually early.” “Sometimes I cook” is also natural.

Conversation

A: What do you do?

B: I work in customer support. What about you?

A: I'm a student, and I'm looking for a part-time job.

B: What kind of work are you looking for?

A: Something where I can practise English.

Practise

1. She ___ (study) every evening.

2. We ___ (prepare) dinner right now.

3. Correct: “He doesn't knows the answer.”

4. Make a question: “You are waiting for someone.”

5. Say three habits and three things happening this week.

Check: 1 studies; 2 are preparing; 3 He doesn't know the answer; 4 Are you waiting for someone? For 5, use the simple for habits and continuous for temporary activities.

07 · Tell people when things happen

MeaningFormExample
Finished past eventpast simpleI called her yesterday.
Activity in progress in the pastwas/were + -ingI was cooking when she called.
Past connected to nowhave/has + past participleI have lost my keys. I can't get in.
A plan or intentionam/is/are going to + verbI'm going to study tonight.
An arranged future eventpresent continuous + future timeI'm meeting Sara at six tomorrow.
A decision now or a promisewill + base verbI'll call you back.

Past simple

Regular verbs often end in -ed: worked, needed. Common verbs are irregular: went, saw, bought. Negative: “I didn't go.” Question: “Did you go?” After did/didn't, use the base form. With be: “I was busy,” “Were you busy?”

Present perfect: use it for a connection, not just “past”

“I have finished the report” emphasizes that it is ready now. “I finished it yesterday” names a finished past time. Avoid “I have finished it yesterday.”

“I have lived here for three years” usually means I still live here. For = duration; since = starting point. “Have you ever visited Sylhet?” asks about experience. “I've never been there.”

Already: “I've already sent it.” Yet: “Have you sent it yet?” / “I haven't sent it yet.” Just: “I've just arrived.” Past simple is also common for some recent events, especially in American English.

Past continuous gives the background

“I was walking home when it started to rain.” The walking was in progress; the rain began during it. “When” does not automatically require a particular tense: choose the form that expresses your meaning.

Practise with one topic

Say what you usually do, what you are doing now, what you did yesterday, what you have done so far this week, and what you are going to do tomorrow. Topic: learning, work, cooking, or exercise.

Correction drill: “I didn't went” → “I didn't go.” “I am living here since 2022” → “I have lived here since 2022” or “I have been living here since 2022.”

08 · Ask questions and handle negatives

Choose the right helper

SentenceQuestionNegative
You are busy.Are you busy?You aren't busy.
She works here.Does she work here?She doesn't work here.
They arrived.Did they arrive?They didn't arrive.
He can help.Can he help?He can't help.
You have finished.Have you finished?You haven't finished.

Question-word pattern: question word + helper + subject + main verb. “Where do you live?” “Why did she leave?” “When will they arrive?”

Subject questions are different: “Who called you?” asks who performed the action. “Who did you call?” asks about the person receiving it.

Ask the question you mean

What = thing/information. Which = choice from a set. Who = person. Whose = ownership. Where = place. When = time. Why = reason. How = method/condition.

“How long does it take?” asks about duration. “How often do you go?” asks about frequency. “How far is it?” asks about distance. “How much is it?” asks about price. “What is she like?” asks about character; “What does she look like?” asks about appearance.

Polite embedded questions

Direct: “Where is the station?” Polite: “Could you tell me where the station is?” In the embedded part, use statement order. Direct: “When does it start?” Polite: “Do you know when it starts?”

For a yes/no question, use if or whether: “Do you know if the shop is open?”

Short answers and follow-ups

“Do you like it?” → “Yes, I do. I especially like the design.” “Can you come?” → “No, I can't, but I can join online.” Add a useful detail instead of stopping at yes or no.

Practice: ask about someone's job, commute, weekend, reason for learning, and next plan. For every answer, ask one related follow-up: “How did you get started?” “What happened next?” “Would you recommend it?”

Common repair: “Why you are late?” → “Why are you late?” “Where you went?” → “Where did you go?”

09 · Articles, quantity, and comparison

A/an, the, or no article?

Use a/an with one nonspecific singular countable noun: “I need a pen.” Choose by sound: an hour, a university. Use the when the listener can identify the thing: “I bought a pen. The pen is blue.”

Use no article for general plural or uncountable nouns: “Books are useful.” “Water is essential.” Compare “The water in this bottle is cold” because this water is specific.

A singular countable noun normally needs something before it: a book, the book, my book, this book. Say “I am a student,” not “I am student.”

Countable and uncountable

Countable: a question, two questions, many questions, a few questions. Uncountable in their usual general senses: information, advice, furniture, equipment, homework, progress. Say “some advice,” “a piece of advice,” “some information.”

Many + plural countable: “How many people?” Much + uncountable: “How much time?” A lot of works with both. Some is common in statements and offers; any is common in questions and negatives: “Would you like some tea?” “Do you have any questions?”

A few = some, often enough; few = not many, often less than wanted. A little = some amount; little = not much. “I have a little time” offers a possibility; “I have little time” emphasizes a shortage.

Compare options

Cheap → cheaper → cheapest. Useful → more useful → most useful. Good → better → best. Bad → worse → worst. “This route is faster than that one.” “This is the most useful option.”

“As … as”: “This one is as good as that one.” Too means more than suitable: “It's too expensive for me.” Enough means sufficient: “It's clear enough.” “We have enough time.”

Practise

1. I need ___ advice. (an / some)

2. She is ___ engineer. (a / an)

3. This is ___ book you recommended. (a / the)

4. We don't have ___ chairs. (enough / much)

5. Make a comparison between two places you know.

Check: 1 some; 2 an; 3 the; 4 enough. Model for 5: “Dhaka is busier than my hometown.”

11 · Requests, permission, advice, and conditions

Match the expression to the relationship

“Send me the file” is direct and may fit a clear instruction. “Could you send me the file, please?” is a useful polite request. “Could you possibly…” is more tentative. More words are not always more polite; clear context and a reasonable tone matter.

PurposePatternExample
Abilitycan + verbI can help you.
PermissionCan/Could I…?Could I use your phone?
RequestCould/Would you…?Could you repeat that?
Adviceshould + verbYou should check the address.
Requirementhave to / must + verbI have to leave by six.
Possibilitymight / may / could + verbIt might rain later.
Preferencewould like / would ratherI'd like some tea. I'd rather stay home.

After can, could, should, must, might, and will, use the base verb: “She can come,” not “can comes” or “can to come.” Would like takes to before a following verb: “I'd like to join.”

Important difference: “You mustn't go” = going is prohibited. “You don't have to go” = going is not necessary. “You should go” = advice.

Say no while keeping the conversation open

“I'm sorry, I can't make it tonight. Would tomorrow work?” “I can help with the first part, but I won't be able to finish the whole thing today.” Avoid promising something you cannot do.

Useful conditionals

General result: “If you heat ice, it melts.” Real future possibility: “If I finish early, I'll call you.” Usually do not put will in the if-clause for this pattern.

Imagined situation: “If I had more time, I would travel more.” This expresses a hypothetical situation, not necessarily past time. Advice: “If I were you, I'd ask for clarification.”

Practice: request help, refuse an invitation, give advice, and explain a condition. Use one reason in each response. Model: “Could you check this sentence? I'm not sure about the verb.”

12 · Connect your ideas without making them tangled

Give a clear message in four parts

Point → reason → example → next step.

“I prefer studying in the morning. I can concentrate better then. For example, I usually remember new phrases more easily before work. So I'm going to study for twenty minutes after breakfast.”

RelationshipConnectorsExample
Additionand, also, in additionIt's affordable, and it's easy to use.
Reasonbecause, sinceI stayed home because I was tired.
Resultso, thereforeI was tired, so I stayed home.
Contrastbut, although, howeverAlthough it was raining, we went out.
Examplefor example, such asTry a small task, such as ordering tea.
Sequencefirst, then, after that, finallyFirst, open the app. Then sign in.

For a straightforward sentence, choose “Because I was tired, I stayed home” or “I was tired, so I stayed home.” Avoid doubling because + so in the same construction. Similarly: “Although it was expensive, I bought it,” not “Although…, but…”.

However usually joins separate statements: “The room is small. However, it is comfortable.” In everyday speech, but is often simpler.

Add detail with relative clauses

“The person who called is my colleague.” “The book that I bought is useful.” “This is the place where we met.” Who describes people; that/which can describe things. For identifying clauses, that is useful for things: “The bus that goes to the airport stops here.”

Keep your listener oriented

“Here's the main issue.” “There are two reasons.” “Let me give you an example.” “What I mean is…” “To sum up…” Use these when they help; do not insert them into every sentence.

Practise

Explain a preference using because. Describe a problem using but. Give instructions using first, then, finally. Describe someone using who.

Model: “I prefer the earlier bus because it's less crowded. It takes a little longer, but I can usually get a seat. My colleague, who lives nearby, takes the same bus.”

ENGLISH FOR EVERYDAY CONFIDENCE · BOOK 112

13 · Keep a conversation moving

A simple rhythm: answer, add, ask

A: Do you enjoy cooking?

B: Yes, I do. I usually cook on weekends. What do you like to make?

A: Mostly simple meals. I tried making soup yesterday.

B: How did it turn out?

Your answer gives information; the extra detail gives the other person something to respond to; the question returns the turn. Avoid making every exchange an interview: share your own reactions too.

Start and end naturally

Start: “Hi, how's your day going?” “How do you know the host?” “Is this your first time here?” For someone you know: “How did your interview go?”

End: “It was good talking to you.” “I need to get back to work, but let's catch up soon.” “Thanks for your help. Have a good day.” Personal questions about age, income, or marriage may feel intrusive in some settings; follow the other person's comfort and the situation.

Repair misunderstandings

If you did not hear: “Sorry, could you say that again?” If you heard but did not understand: “What does ‘reschedule’ mean here?” If details matter: “Did you say fifteen or fifty?” To confirm: “So we're meeting at four, right?”

If you used the wrong word: “Sorry, I meant Thursday, not Tuesday.” If you need time: “Let me think for a moment.” If you do not know a word: describe its purpose, appearance, or location. “It's the thing you use to charge your phone.”

Disagree without attacking

“I see your point, but I think…” “That could work. My concern is…” “I agree with the first part. I'm less sure about…” Give a reason or propose an alternative.

Practise for three minutes

Topic: the best way to learn a skill. Include one opening, two follow-up questions, one example, one polite disagreement, one clarification, and one closing. Replay your recording. Did you respond to what was said, or only wait to say your planned sentences?

14 · Pronunciation for clear communication

Your aim is intelligibility: people understand you without unnecessary effort. You do not need to erase your Bangla accent. Different Bangla speakers have different habits, so practise the sounds that actually cause difficulty for you.

Check these sound contrasts

ContrastExamplesHow to practise
/v/ and /w/vine / wine; vest / westFor v, upper teeth touch lower lip with voice. For w, round the lips without tooth-lip contact.
/ɪ/ and /iː/ship / sheep; live / leaveListen for vowel quality as well as length; imitate a clear model.
/θ/ and /ð/thin / then; teeth / breatheLet air pass with tongue near or lightly between the teeth; add voice for /ð/.
Final consonantsback / bag; rice / riseKeep the ending audible enough to distinguish the word.

The symbols are sound labels, not a complete pronunciation course. Listen to the example words using English read-aloud or a clear speaker; then record yourself. A text-only description cannot replace hearing the contrast.

Keep consonant groups together

Practise “school,” “street,” “next,” and “asked” slowly. Avoid adding an extra vowel if it changes the word: school, not “is-kool.” Build backward: ask → asked; next → next day. Natural speech may simplify some groups, but clarity comes first.

Endings carry meaning

-ed can sound like /t/ in worked, /d/ in played, or /ɪd/ in wanted. Add an extra syllable after /t/ or /d/: wanted, needed. Plural/third-person -s can sound like /s/ in books, /z/ in bags, or /ɪz/ in buses.

Stress and thought groups

Say im-POR-tant, de-CIDE, de-CI-sion, com-MU-ni-cate. Capital letters mark the stressed syllable, not spelling. In a sentence, emphasize the message words: “I NEED the FILE by FRIDAY.”

Read in groups: “After work / I'm meeting a friend / near the station.” Pause between ideas. Compare “I wanted the BLUE one” with “I wanted the blue ONE”: stress changes the focus.

Daily drill: listen to two lines, mark pauses, repeat slowly, record, compare, and say them again at a comfortable pace. Do not race the recording.

ENGLISH FOR EVERYDAY CONFIDENCE · BOOK 114

15 · Train your listening with the included scripts

Use the four scripts on page 20. Ask a partner to read them, or use a device's English text-to-speech/read-aloud feature. Select an English voice. Synthetic speech is useful for practice but does not represent every real accent or conversational style.

A listening cycle

1. Hide the text. Listen once for the situation and main point.

2. Listen again for names, times, numbers, reasons, and required actions.

3. Say what you understood before opening the transcript.

4. Read the transcript and locate the parts you missed.

5. Listen while reading, then listen again without reading.

6. Retell the message in your own words.

Do not try to translate every word while listening. Missing one word does not mean losing the entire message. Listen for stressed information and the speaker's purpose.

Dictation and shadowing

Dictation: play one short sentence, pause, and write what you heard. Compare it with the transcript. Mark missing endings or short words such as a, the, to, and have.

Shadowing: after understanding the meaning, repeat a short section just after the speaker. Start with one sentence. Copy pauses and emphasis. Then stop the audio and say the same idea naturally in your own words.

Contractions you need to recognize

I'm = I am; you're = you are; don't = do not; can't = cannot; I'll = I will; we've = we have. She's can mean she is or she has: “She's tired”; “She's finished.” I'd can mean I would or I had: “I'd like some tea”; “I'd already left.” The following words help you decide.

Informal speech may reduce “going to” to a sound like “gonna” and “want to” to “wanna.” Learn to recognize these; standard spelling is the safest choice for ordinary writing.

Track understanding

For each script, record: main point correct? key details correct? action correct? Do not judge success only by the number of individual words you recognize.

Transfer task: after practising a script, change its time, place, and reason. Have a partner read the new version, or listen to it later when you no longer remember the details.

16 · Tell stories and explain experiences

A useful story structure

Situation → problem → action → result → reflection.

“Last Saturday, I went to visit a friend in another part of the city. While I was waiting for the bus, I realized that I had left my wallet at home. I called my brother, and he brought it to me. I arrived thirty minutes late, but my friend was understanding. Since then, I've checked my bag before leaving home.”

Notice the job of each form: went gives the main event; was waiting gives the background; had left places one past event before another; have checked connects the lesson to now.

Make it easier to follow

Set the time: yesterday, last month, a few years ago. Name the people before using he, she, or they repeatedly. Use then, after that, eventually, and in the end where they clarify the sequence. Include enough detail to explain the problem; remove details that distract.

“Eventually” means after some time or difficulty. “Finally” often marks the last step or relief after waiting. “In the end” introduces the final outcome.

Expand an answer without memorizing a speech

Question: “How was your weekend?”

Short: “It was good.”

Expanded: “It was relaxing. I visited my cousin on Saturday, and we cooked dinner together. I needed a break after a busy week.”

Question: “Tell me about a challenge.”

Response: “I had to finish a task with very little time. I divided it into smaller steps and asked a colleague to check the important parts. We finished on time, and I learned to ask for help earlier.”

Three speaking tasks

1. Tell a one-minute story about a small mistake. Include what happened and what you learned.

2. Tell it again in thirty seconds. Keep the main events.

3. Tell it a third time and answer: “Why did that happen?” “How did you feel?” “What would you do differently?”

Record the first and third versions. Compare organization and clarity, not just speed. Reusing the same story helps you focus on language rather than inventing new content every time.

17 · Everyday conversation: solve the situation

At a shop

Customer: I'm looking for a phone charger. Does this work with this model?

Assistant: Yes, it does. Would you like to try it?

Customer: Yes, please. How much is it?

Assistant: It's eight hundred taka.

Customer: Thanks. Could I have a receipt as well?

Complication: it does not work after purchase. “I bought this yesterday, but it isn't working. Could you check it? I'd like a replacement if possible.” Describe the problem before proposing a solution.

Making an appointment

A: I'd like to make an appointment for Thursday.

B: We have a slot at three. Does that work for you?

A: I have another meeting then. Is anything available after four?

B: We can offer you 4:30.

A: That works. Could you confirm the address, please?

Complication: “I'm sorry, something has come up. Could we reschedule for next week?” Give a new option when possible.

Asking for directions

A: Excuse me, how do I get to the station?

B: Go straight ahead, then turn left at the traffic lights.

A: Is it within walking distance?

B: Yes, about ten minutes on foot.

A: So I turn left at the lights, right? Thank you.

Inviting and responding

“Would you like to have lunch on Friday?” → “I'd love to. What time?”

“I can't this Friday, but I'm free on Saturday. Would that work?”

“Thanks for inviting me. I'm not sure yet. Can I let you know tomorrow?”

Practice method

Read one dialogue aloud. Close the book. Repeat the situation with a different product, day, location, or price. On the third attempt, add a complication: the item is unavailable, the time changes, or you misunderstand a direction.

Success means: you stated your need, understood or checked the response, and agreed on a next step. It does not require using the exact model wording.

18 · Workplace and online communication

Give a useful update

Use completed → current → blocked → next.

“I've finished the first draft. I'm checking the examples now. I'm waiting for confirmation about the deadline. Once I have that, I'll send the final version.”

If a deadline is at risk: “I may need an extra day because two details are still missing. I can send the completed sections today. Would tomorrow afternoon work for the final version?” State the impact and propose a realistic option.

Participate in a meeting

Enter: “Could I add something?” Clarify: “When you say ‘ready,’ do you mean ready for review or ready to publish?” Check responsibility: “Who will handle the next step?” Confirm: “So I'll update the document, and you'll review it by Thursday.”

Online: “Your audio is breaking up.” “I think you're on mute.” “Could you share your screen?” “I lost connection for a moment. Could you repeat the last point?”

Ask for help with context

Vague: “It doesn't work.”

Useful: “I'm trying to sign in, but I get an error after entering my password. I've already tried resetting it. Could you help me check what to do next?”

Pattern: goal + problem + what you tried + specific request.

Receive feedback

“Thanks for pointing that out.” “Could you show me an example?” “Let me check that I've understood.” “I'll revise that section and send it again.” You can disagree respectfully: “I understand the concern. My reason for doing it this way was… Could we compare both options?”

Interview answer framework

Present situation → relevant experience → fit for the role. “I work in customer support. In my current role, I explain solutions and follow up on unresolved issues. I'm interested in this position because I enjoy helping people and want to develop my communication skills.” Use true details; do not memorize claims that do not fit you.

Practice: give a 45-second update, ask one clarification question, and write a three-line follow-up recording the decision, owner, and deadline.

19 · Write clear messages and emails

Build a message around the reader's next action

Purpose → necessary context → request/action → time → polite close.

Subject: Request to move Thursday's meeting

Hi Sara,

Could we move our meeting from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. on Thursday? I have another appointment in the morning. If 2 p.m. doesn't work, I'm also available after 3:30.

Please let me know which time suits you.

Thanks,

Rafi

Three reusable messages

Request: “Hi Nila, could you send me the updated file by 4 p.m. today? I need it for tomorrow's meeting. Thanks.”

Apology and repair: “I'm sorry I sent the wrong attachment. Please use the corrected file attached to this message. Thank you for your patience.”

Follow-up: “Hi Arif, I'm following up on my message about the appointment. Could you confirm whether Friday at three is available? Thanks.”

Choose a suitable tone

A friend: “Can you send me the photo?” A colleague: “Could you send me the updated version?” An unfamiliar recipient: “Hello, I'd like to ask about…” “Dear…” is appropriate for some formal letters; it is not necessary for every chat message.

Avoid unclear references such as “Please do the needful” when you can name the action: “Please review the attached form and confirm the address.” Common regional expressions can be understandable; this course prioritizes wording that travels easily across settings.

Edit in four passes

1. Meaning: is the purpose clear in the first sentence?

2. Action: does the reader know what to do and by when?

3. Grammar: check verbs, articles, plurals, and pronouns.

4. Mechanics: capitalize I, names, days, and English; add punctuation and paragraphs.

Do not join complete sentences with only a comma: “I was late, the bus broke down.” Write “I was late because the bus broke down” or use two sentences.

Practice: write a rescheduling request, an apology for a delay, and a short progress update. Keep each under 80 words. Compare with the models on page 26.

21 · Read for meaning, then summarize

Reading passage · A manageable habit

Mina wanted to improve her English, but she kept changing her study plan. One week, she memorized a long list of words. The next week, she watched difficult videos and understood very little. She felt busy, but she rarely used the same language twice.

She decided to make her routine smaller. Each morning, she chose five useful expressions and wrote a true sentence with each one. On the bus, she tried to remember the expressions without looking. In the evening, she recorded a one-minute message about her day.

At first, she stopped frequently. She knew what she wanted to say in Bangla, but she could not always find the English words. Instead of restarting the recording, she described the missing word or used a simpler sentence. She checked two mistakes after each recording and tried again.

After several weeks, some expressions came to her more easily. She still made mistakes, and unfamiliar conversations were difficult. However, she could explain her routine and ask follow-up questions with less preparation. She realized that progress did not always mean learning something new. Sometimes it meant using familiar language more confidently.

Questions

1. Why did Mina's original routine give her little repeated practice?

2. What did she do in the morning, on the bus, and in the evening?

3. What did she do when she could not remember a word?

4. Does the passage claim she became fluent? Give evidence.

5. In “some expressions came to her more easily,” does came describe physical movement?

6. Summarize the passage in three sentences without copying whole sentences.

Read actively

First read: identify the main idea. Second read: underline the action and the reason in each paragraph. Third read: notice useful phrases such as “without looking,” “instead of,” and “with less preparation.”

Guess an unknown meaning from context before checking Book 2. It is fine to leave a nonessential word unresolved on the first reading. Do not interrupt every sentence to translate it.

Speaking transfer: explain one part of Mina's routine that you could use, one part you would change, and why. Answers and a model summary are on page 26.

Four extensions you will hear often

Past perfect: had + participle for an earlier past event. “When I arrived, the meeting had already started.” Use it when the earlier sequence needs to be clear.

Passive: be + participle when the action or receiver matters. “The package was delivered yesterday.” “English is spoken here.” Use active wording when it is clearer: “Sara sent the file.”

When you get stuck

“I know words but cannot speak.” Practise whole phrases and repeat the same speaking task three times. Use simpler words while keeping the message moving.

“I understand reading but not speech.” Use short audio sections, hide the transcript first, then compare. Focus on contractions, word boundaries, and key details.

“I make the same grammar mistakes.” Pick one pattern, write five true examples, and deliberately use it in tomorrow's recording. Review the error log weekly.

“I translate everything.” Reuse familiar situations until the response becomes easier: greetings, explaining your day, making requests. Move from Bangla cue → English phrase to situation → English response.

“I forget vocabulary.” Reduce the number of new entries and increase retrieval. Recognition is easier than recall; cover the answer and actually produce it.

The next four weeks
